# Order timing passes; news timing leaks value

The fund's last-minute execution is close to what the literature and careful practitioners recommend for a small book scored at the official close, and on the record it costs nothing measurable: **150 pre-close market orders all filled in full on their first attempt, at a signed cost of +0.6 bp against the official close (standard error 0.8 bp)**, and **no stop or target decision taken on close−3-minute prices would have come out differently at the close**. The real departures from best practice sit upstream and in the scorecard. The news vendor reaches the kill checks **two to three hours late**, so the nominal close−20-minute news cutoff behaves like a cutoff near close−2.5 hours, and articles the vendor serves late are never checked at all. Weekly cohorts enter **22–23 hours** after their information cutoff, and nobody reads those names' news in between. The academic evidence says large-cap news is largely priced within the day, so these delays give up whatever drift remains, although the fund's own drift study finds little left to lose. On evaluation, the go-live gate scores median absolute slippage, which measures noise rather than cost, and it currently reports 0% of weeks executed because it reads retired files. The model books charge 5 bp a side against a measured 0.8–0.9 bp, which at the book's turnover of roughly **70 times NAV a year understates the scored return by about 3% a year**, and the shadow control that Protocol 7B is judged against checks its own positions' news hours after the close, giving it a better information set than the live book. Most fixes are measurement and qualify as operational; anything that changes which news reaches a decision, when a decision is made or how the book is scored needs a new pre-registered protocol or a dated amendment written before outcomes are seen, and the one execution change with real money behind it (deciding by 3:49 pm ET so orders can go to the closing auction) belongs to the go-live decision, because the paper engine cannot simulate the auction.

## The close is the cheapest place to trade, and the fund trades there

Every strand of evidence points a large-cap book toward the close. The US closing auction grew from **3.1% of daily dollar volume in 2010 to 7.5% in 2018** ([Bogousslavsky & Muravyev](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/6310c0b9bb63a25599f4418c/t/634ffc92f81e226b2c30654f/1666186387645/who-trades-at-the-close_June2021.pdf)), to about 9% on a typical 2024 day ([BMLL](https://www.bmlltech.com/news/market-insight/into-the-close-unpacking-u-s-closing-auction-dynamics-and-the-impact-of-the-russell-reconstitution)) and to **12–13% in 2025** in Instinet's data ([State Street](https://www.ssga.com/us/en/institutional/insights/how-passive-investing-reshaping-microstructure)), and BestEx counts about **a third of S&P 500 trades in the final ten minutes** ([Bloomberg](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2024-04-29/stocks-trade-for-390-minutes-a-day-increasingly-only-10-matter)). Spreads no longer follow the old U-shape; they narrow into the close, and the day's narrowest quotes coincide with the end-of-day volume spike ([Jiang & Yao](https://polysz.cuhk.edu.cn/zh-hans/node/12827); [Cboe](https://www.cboe.com/insights/posts/the-impact-closing-auctions-have-on-volumes)). Price impact is lower in the closing auction than in continuous trading for everything except Nasdaq micro-caps: **3.2 bp against 6.4 bp** for large NYSE stocks at 0.5% of daily volume ([Goyal, Jegadeesh & Wu](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-financial-and-quantitative-analysis/article/price-impact-in-closing-auctions-opening-auctions-and-continuous-markets-a-benchmark-for-cost-of-trading-on-anomalies/0F72910A79C5B42CF6E85F55164CE846)), and BestEx finds the final hour consistently the cheapest time to work a fixed-size order ([BestEx](https://www.bestexresearch.com/insights/the-time-of-day-effect-a-breakthrough-in-trading-cost-optimization)).

The open is the mirror image. Opening auctions clear a median **0.9% of daily volume** with much larger impact (Goyal, Jegadeesh & Wu, above); effective spreads for large stocks in the first fifteen minutes widened by about 10 bp over 2010–2018 as volume migrated to the close (Bogousslavsky & Muravyev, above); and opening prices of attention-grabbing stocks are systematically too high and reverse during the day ([Berkman et al.](https://ideas.repec.org/a/cup/jfinqa/v47y2012i04p715-741_00.html)). Forum traders say the same in plainer words, reporting thin, partial fills at the open and routing larger orders to the close ([Elite Trader](https://www.elitetrader.com/et/threads/your-thoughts-on-placing-market-orders-at-open-and-close.291673)).

Timing also decides which returns a book owns. Momentum-type returns accrue overnight: over 1993–2013, momentum earned a CAPM alpha of **0.98% a month close-to-open and −0.02% open-to-close** ([Lou, Polk & Skouras](https://personal.lse.ac.uk/polk/research/TugOfWar.pdf)), and in news-momentum data about 40% of the five-day drift arrived in the first overnight gap after the news day ([Jiang, Li & Wang](https://www.villanova.edu/dam/villanova/VSB/assets/marc/marc2018/SSRN-id2667658.pdf)). Mispricing-type returns run the other way, because arbitrageurs unwind before the overnight period and anomaly portfolios do badly in the last half-hour ([Bogousslavsky](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jfinec/v141y2021i1p172-194.html)). A close entry therefore buys the next overnight return, which suits continuation signals and gives mispricing signals a slightly better entry price. In the fund's 11 Protocol 7 sessions, held positions did slightly worse between the decision and the close on 7 days, about −15 bp of NAV in total (−3.4% a year if it persisted). That is the direction end-of-day unwinding predicts, but the same positions recur from day to day, and the move is not a cost of the timing design, since the positions are held through the close either way (`fund-data: lifecycle/live/*.json`, `lifecycle/returns.csv`).

The fund sits squarely in this consensus. Under Protocol 7, the rules under which the lifecycle book has traded the paper account since 15 October 2026 (each position has its own horizon, a stop and a target on hedged residual return, and LLM "kill checks" that close it when news ends its thesis; Protocol 7B, live since 28 October, keeps the same timing), every trade goes out in the last minute before the close and every position is marked at the official close ([PROTOCOL_fund.md, "Protocol 7"](research/PROTOCOL_fund.md)). Weekly cohorts enter at the close of the first trading day after the as-of Wednesday, normally Thursday ([fund/lifecycle.py](../fund/lifecycle.py)). The strongest industry alternative is AQR's process: form targets on the prior close and trade the next day with patient algorithms, accepting an implementation shortfall that averaged **11 bp** on $1.7 trillion of live trades ([Frazzini, Israel & Moskowitz](https://spinup-000d1a-wp-offload-media.s3.amazonaws.com/faculty/wp-content/uploads/sites/3/2021/08/Trading-Cost.pdf)). Practitioners with slow signals deliberately build a one-day lag into their backtests ([Carver](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=udBUed_tSVs)), and Allocate Smartly found that such a lag cost monthly asset-allocation strategies just **0.02% a year** on average but hurt badly in the March 2020 crash ([Allocate Smartly](https://allocatesmartly.com/adding-a-1-day-lag-when-executing-taa-strategies)). News signals decay over hours and days, not months, so trading at the same close is the right design for this book; the open question is how old its information is by the time it trades.

## Deciding at close−3 minutes rules out the auction, at about a basis point a side

For an order judged against the official close, theory and practice agree: send it to the closing auction. In a mean-variance model with the close as benchmark, an order with no impact on the auction price should trade only in the auction ([Frei et al.](https://www.math.ualberta.ca/~cfrei/CFCY2018.pdf)); an Almgren–Chriss treatment of "target close" puts most of the order there and trades in the continuous market only what the auction cannot absorb ([Labadie & Lehalle](https://arxiv.org/html/1205.3482v6)); agency close algorithms "set aside as many shares as possible" for the auction ([Proof Trading](https://medium.com/prooftrading/the-close-algo-fdf0f56afa7a)). ITG puts the corollary bluntly: auction fills have zero slippage to the close by construction, so close-benchmarked flow gains nothing from trading in the last fifteen minutes except extra spread and impact ([ITG](https://ptacts.uspto.gov/ptacts/public-informations/petitions/1519335/download-documents?artifactId=sHrUVQMi0N7XVM_lDc_wXNqYL4jCaW5Y6jb7itY5YOo9-lizK3df2Ew)).

The catch is the cutoff. NYSE accepts, modifies and cancels on-close orders only until **3:50 pm ET** ([NYSE](https://WWW.NYSE.COM/publicdocs/nyse/markets/nyse/NYSE_Opening_and_Closing_Auctions_Fact_Sheet.pdf)); Nasdaq takes market-on-close until 3:55 and limit-on-close until 3:58 but freezes cancellations at 3:50 ([Nasdaq](https://nasdaqtrader.com/content/productsservices/trading/crosses/openclose_faqs.pdf)); and Alpaca rejects every `cls` order submitted after **3:50 pm** ([Alpaca](https://docs.alpaca.markets/us/docs/orders-at-alpaca)). A decision at 3:57 cannot reach the auction by any route the fund has. Practitioners solve this by deciding early. QuantConnect staff build a bar 20 minutes before the close and send market-on-close ([QuantConnect](https://www.quantconnect.com/forum/discussion/18821/timing-issue-using-market-orders-on-daily-resolution)); a much-cited Quant Stack Exchange answer accepts the resulting sizing error as noise "in either direction" ([Quant SE](https://quant.stackexchange.com/questions/8595/backtesting-can-you-buy-sell-at-open-and-closing-prices)); Concretum submits market-on-close at 3:49:55 and keeps a fallback market order ready for 3:59:58 ([Concretum](https://concretumgroup.com/building-reliable-trading-systems-algorithmic-trading-automation)). Once the decision is fixed at close−3 minutes, a close-aware algorithm reduces to what the fund already does: one marketable order in the continuous market just before the bell.

The fund arrived at this path out of necessity, without a comparison. On 1 October all 20 market-on-close orders expired on the paper account and only 9.7% of shares filled, because Alpaca's paper engine has no closing auction ([PROTOCOL_fund.md, execution note to Amendment 3](research/PROTOCOL_fund.md)); Alpaca's documentation and staff confirm that paper fills `cls` orders against the quote with random partial fills ([Alpaca paper trading](https://docs.alpaca.markets/docs/paper-trading); [Alpaca forum](https://forum.alpaca.markets/t/paper-moc-orders-partially-fill-remainder-expires-same-setup-on-live-works/19155)). On 9 October the owner made the pre-close send the method for a live account too, and no document weighs it against a live auction route ([PROTOCOL_fund.md](research/PROTOCOL_fund.md)).

On the record the send is accurate and effectively free. Across 150 pre-close fills ($468k traded, 7–29 October), slippage against the official close has a **signed mean of +0.57 bp (standard error 0.78)** and a notional-weighted mean of +0.77 bp, about $36 in total; the median absolute gap is 5.3 bp and the 90th percentile 14 bp. Against the decision price the median is 0.00 bp and the mean +1.04 bp (`fund-data: execution/fills.csv` joined to `lifecycle/live/*.json`). The largest outlier was favourable (the fund sold CEG at 276.52 and it closed at 275.00), and on $25–35 stocks a one-cent tick alone is 3–4 bp, which is why the noise roughly doubles there. The protocol justified the send with the **1.7 bp** median gap between the auction print and the 4 pm midquote for large caps (Bogousslavsky & Muravyev, above). The measured dispersion is three times that, because a fill at close−54 s also carries the last minute of continuous trading, which is mostly a lasting price move: only 19% of the 3:55–4:00 move reverses by the next morning. The understatement is harmless, since the dispersion is zero-mean tracking noise rather than cost and sits well inside the gate's 10 bp. The decision side is equally robust. From the close−3-minute price to the official close, held names moved a median **8.3 bp**, and on the hedged residual return that drives the stops and targets the gap never exceeded **0.07 W**, where W is a position's volatility unit (stop at −2 W, target at +3 W). Across 131 position-days and 91 logged actions **no decision would have differed** at the close, and the nearest call was 0.23 W from a stop (`fund-data: lifecycle/live/*.json`, `lifecycle/returns.csv`). The Quant SE caveat, that proxy error is noise only if the decision is independent of the last minutes' price action, applies to stop exits; the one stop taken so far was far through its barrier at both times.

What would the auction add? About a half-spread per side, plus the removal of the tracking noise. The measured Protocol 7 cost against the close is about +0.9 bp per side notional-weighted in paper, in line with large-cap realised half-spreads near 1.1 bp (Bogousslavsky & Muravyev, above) and quoted spreads of about 3.7 bp for the 100 largest S&P 500 names ([Nasdaq](https://www.nasdaq.com/articles/sampling-sp-500-minimize-spreads)). That sounds trivial, but this book turns over heavily. In the ten sessions after the switch-over (16–29 October) it traded about **28% of NAV a day, roughly 70 times NAV a year** (my arithmetic on `fund-data: execution/fills.csv`), so **each basis point per side is worth about 0.7% of NAV a year**. Routing to the auction is worth up to about that much. It is an upper bound, because the paper estimate is not statistically different from zero and live price improvement would shrink it ([Alpaca forum](https://forum.alpaca.markets/t/does-a-paid-account-give-more-accurate-paper-market-fills/14303)). The price of the change is deciding by about 3:49 pm, eleven minutes earlier. Scaling the measured drift by the square root of time, stop/target checks would then run on prices that drift a typical 16 bp to the close, still at most about 4% of W, and a news cutoff ten minutes earlier is irrelevant next to the vendor lag described below. Two facts keep this a go-live question rather than a fix for today: it cannot be tested on paper, and Alpaca's own statements conflict on live auction access. The order documentation implies standard accounts can use `cls`, while a June 2026 staff reply says it is "not generally supported in live trading unless one has chosen the elite smart router" ([Alpaca forum](https://forum.alpaca.markets/t/paper-moc-orders-partially-fill-remainder-expires-same-setup-on-live-works/19155); [Alpaca Elite](https://alpaca.markets/elite)).

One order at one fixed minute is the right shape for this benchmark. Corey Hoffstein's contrary case deserves a hearing: executing the same rules at randomly chosen times produced a **3.5x** spread in terminal wealth, so he recommends spreading execution over a window "and let the randomness average out" ([Hoffstein](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/coreyhoffstein_i-havent-written-about-rebalance-timing-activity-7443828719594483712-BjaL)). That argument is about economic return. This book is scored at the official close, and the theory above says splitting adds tracking error against that benchmark without saving any impact on $0.2k–6k orders. The fixed, unrandomised burst at close−60 s would be a predictable footprint at institutional size and is irrelevant here. Moving the send to close−30 s would shave drift noise and moving it to close−120 s would widen the retry window, but neither has a measurable expected benefit.

Operationally the design already follows the practitioner checklist. Concretum's production lessons ([Concretum](https://concretumgroup.com/building-reliable-trading-systems-algorithmic-trading-automation)) call for an exchange calendar instead of a hard-coded 4:00 pm, clock-offset correction, idempotent order IDs, reconciliation against the broker and alerts rather than silent failure, and the fund has all five ([fund/broker.py](../fund/broker.py), [fund/lifecycle.py](../fund/lifecycle.py)). Its resend loop also guards against a documented failure in which an exchange wiped on-close orders and required resubmission ([Traders Magazine](https://www.tradersmagazine.com/departments/technology/update-nyse-resumes-trading-after-solving-trading-glitch)). If the primary auction fails after 3:00 pm, the official close becomes the last five minutes' volume-weighted price, a window the close−54 s fill sits inside ([Nasdaq](https://www.nasdaqtrader.com/TraderNews.aspx?id=ETA2025-98)). Several conditions have never been exercised: the retry path, partial fills and a cancelled residual; a limit-up/limit-down pause, which for Tier 1 stocks needs an extreme move because the bands double to 10% after 3:35 pm ([FINRA](https://www.finra.org/investors/insights/guardrails-market-volatility)); an order-entry outage like Nasdaq's of 13 December 2023 ([Fortune](https://fortune.com/2023/12/13/nasdaq-error-affects-trades)); winter time, which starts Monday 2 November with the close at 21:00 UTC; and a half-day. Friday 27 November closes at 1:00 pm ET ([NYSE](https://www.nyse.com/markets/hours-calendars)), and because Thanksgiving closes Thursday and the code enters a cohort on the next trading day ([fund/lifecycle.py](../fund/lifecycle.py)), it is also the entry day for the cohort decided Wednesday 25 November. That day is the first time a half-day, winter time and a cohort entry coincide, and that week's information delay stretches to 44 hours. The direction of the paper-to-live gap is unresolved: live accounts get price improvement that paper never gives, and pay latency that paper does not simulate, and for orders this small both forces are around a basis point.

## News reaches the decision hours late, and some of it never arrives

The literature on how fast news is priced is unambiguous for large caps. In the 50 most liquid S&P 100 stocks over 2008–2020, about **70% of price discovery occurs within five minutes** of an after-hours earnings release, and trading the surprise loses its edge with delays beyond five seconds ([Christensen, Timmermann & Veliyev](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.08962)). On Dow Jones Newswires text, stocks above the NYSE median respond **11 bp on the first day after news and nothing after** ([Ke, Kelly & Xiu](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w26186/w26186.pdf)). GPT-4 headline signals are "heavily concentrated in smaller stocks", with value-weighted Sharpe ratios of 0.56 for overnight news and 1.28 for intraday news, and the overnight strategy's Sharpe ratio fell from 6.54 in late 2021 to 1.22 in early 2024 ([Lopez-Lira & Tang](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.07619)). On S&P 500 headlines specifically, a GPT signal earned an insignificant **3.8 bp a day (t = 0.92)** out of sample ([Glasserman & Lin](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.17322)), and post-earnings drift has been absent for everything but micro-caps since about 2005 ([Martineau](https://cfr.ivo-welch.info/published/papers/martineau2021rest.pdf)). Across three studies, a one-day delay costs a daily text signal roughly **25–65% of its tradable drift**, while weekly-aggregated news keeps predicting returns for 13 weeks, so for such signals a day costs much less ([Heston & Sinha](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econresdata/feds/2016/files/2016048pap.pdf)).

On paper the fund's design is conservative and realistic. Academic close-entry designs often count news up to 4:00 pm as tradable at the 4:00 pm close, which assumes zero processing time ([He et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.21206); Glasserman & Lin, above), while Ke, Kelly & Xiu deliberately impose a 30-minute gap. A close−20-minute news cutoff, a close−3-minute decision and a close−54-second fill sit between the two. The checks finish 71–279 seconds after the cutoff, leaving more than a quarter of an hour of margin (`fund-data: lifecycle/news/*.json`).

In practice the vendor's clock sets the cutoff, not the design's. Across 11 live sessions and 154 position checks, the newest article any check saw was stamped a **median 2 h 49 min before the cutoff** and never less than 95 minutes before it. No same-day article fell in the last twenty minutes and **only 1.7%** fell in the two hours before, whereas shadow checks run five hours after the close found about a quarter of same-day articles in those two hours. The data vendor (Finnhub) evidently serves company news 1.5–3 hours after its timestamp. Because each check's window starts exactly at the previous check's cutoff ([fund/lifecycle.py](../fund/lifecycle.py)), an article stamped at 2 pm but served at 5 pm falls before the next window opens and is **never shown to any check**. For articles that do arrive, the median wait from publication to the decision that can act on them is **6.8 hours**, rising to 22 hours for after-close news and 36 hours over weekends; 54 of 154 checks also hit the 25-article cap, whose drops are not logged (`fund-data: lifecycle/news/*.prompts.jsonl.gz`). Practitioners complain that retail RSS feeds lag five to ten minutes behind the wire ([r/algotrading](https://www.reddit.com/r/algotrading/comments/1ozljry/news_feed_latency)); the fund's lag is two orders of magnitude worse, and the twenty-minute buffer is not the binding constraint. The repository's own guardrail checks coverage "against what was expected, not against zero" ([CLAUDE.md](../CLAUDE.md)), yet nothing checks how recent the news is.

What this costs is bounded but not measurable from the records. A thesis-ending story that arrives late waits about a day, which for a large cap gives up roughly the 11 bp day-one residual. One that never arrives gives up the exit entirely, and no record counts how often that happens. The steelman for lateness comes from 2023–2026 data in which soft news (product launches, leadership changes, macro read-throughs) reverses over the following weeks while quantified news (earnings, guidance) continues ([Kargarzadeh et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.14014)). For soft-news theses, reacting late can beat reacting fast.

The weekly cohort adds a second, larger delay. The desks read news to Wednesday 22:00 UTC ([fund/common.py](../fund/common.py)) and enter at Thursday's close, **22 hours later in summer and 23 in winter**. For a new cohort's names, nothing reads company news between the Wednesday cutoff and Thursday's close−20-minute mark, so a Thursday-morning story that contradicts the thesis cannot stop the entry and can only trigger an exit a day later. The desk review of 14 October rated this "high severity": the classifier dated the driving events one to five trading days before entry, and 6 of 20 views were stale ([desk review](research/desk_review_2026-10.md)). The fund's own drift study, on 8,924 S&P 500 news-gap events in 2012–2025, shows little to lose. From the Thursday entry the drift was **+4, +12 and +13 bp per event at one to three sessions (t ≤ 1.5)** against about 10 bp of round-trip cost, no less than the +5, +8 and +6 bp measured from the news-day close ([technicals_backtest.md](research/technicals_backtest.md)). For scale, cohorts entered 38–64% of NAV a week in October (`fund-data: lifecycle/live/2026-10-15, -22, -29.json`); if the literature's large-cap day-one residual of 5–11 bp were lost in full on every entry, the weekly delay would cost about **1.5–3% a year**. That is an upper bound, and the fund's drift profile says the true figure is far smaller.

The shadow books built to measure this delay cannot answer yet. Over three scored weeks, a book holding the same weekly weights from Wednesday's close ran 0.21% a week ahead of the Thursday-close book and a Thursday-open book ran 0.33% a week ahead, gaps that annualise to roughly 11% and 19% a year and are pure noise at three weeks (`fund-data: performance/weekly_books.csv`). The owner's rule defers any verdict to about mid-April 2027. The shadows also hold the weekly book's weights, not the lifecycle positions, so nothing yet measures the timing of the daily kill checks and stop/target exits.

## The scorecard measures noise, misreads the record and handicaps the fund

Industry measures execution with implementation shortfall: the return of a paper portfolio traded at decision prices minus the return of the real one. Wagner's version splits it into delay (decision price to arrival), trading (arrival to fill) and opportunity cost on unfilled shares, charged on the intended notional so that failed fills cannot flatter the average ([Hasbrouck](https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~jhasbrou/Teaching/POST%202015%20Fall/classNotes/STPPTradingCosts.pdf); [blotter](https://github.com/braverock/blotter/blob/master/R/impShortfall.R)). For a book scored at the close, standard practice reports three numbers per fill. Shortfall against the decision price measures economic cost; slippage against the arrival midpoint measures what order handling controls; and slippage against the official close measures whether live trading matches a backtest that fills at the close ([Talos](https://www.talos.com/insights/execution-insights-through-transaction-cost-analysis-tca-benchmarks-and-slippage); ITG, above). The fund computes only the third. It stores the decision price with every order without using it ([fund/execute.py](../fund/execute.py)), and it records no arrival quote.

The go-live gate scores the wrong statistic. It requires median absolute slippage against the close of at most 10 bp ([PROTOCOL_fund.md](research/PROTOCOL_fund.md)), which measures dispersion. Proof Trading calls arrival slippage "notoriously noisy" ([Proof](https://prooftrading.com/docs/tca-2025-full-year.pdf)), and in AQR's data the mean, median and value-weighted mean of costs differ by a factor of 2.5 (Frazzini, Israel & Moskowitz, above). Costs add up in means, so the cost estimate is the **signed, notional-weighted mean with a confidence interval clustered by day**, with dispersion reported beside it. With per-fill noise of about 10 bp, 150 fills pin the mean to about ±1.5 bp: enough to show 5 bp a side is conservative, not enough to detect a 1–2 bp difference between two execution methods, which needs roughly 200 to 800 independent fills, and more because same-day fills share the market's last-minute move ([Markov](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1904.01566)). The gate also misreads the record: it reports "weeks fully executed 0%" because it reads only the retired weekly execution files, while every Protocol 7 order lives in the lifecycle records ([fund/score.py](../fund/score.py); `fund-data: performance/gate.md`).

The cost assumption handicaps the scored record. Every model book charges 5 bp a side, the hedge included ([fund/lifecycle.py](../fund/lifecycle.py); [fund/score.py](../fund/score.py)), against the 0.8–0.9 bp measured in paper. At October's turnover that is about **3.5% a year charged against about 0.6% a year incurred**, so the scored record understates the book's return by roughly **3% a year** relative to an S&P 500 yardstick that pays no such charge. Recorded days are never recomputed ([CLAUDE.md](../CLAUDE.md)), so the remedy is to report the measured-cost line beside the model rather than rewrite it; the broker account already carries real paper costs. For a live account in this universe, 5 bp stays conservative: half-spreads for orders this small run about 0.5–2 bp for most S&P 500 names and a few basis points for the least liquid (Nasdaq, above). AQR's 9–11 bp institutional averages are the wrong comparison for $3k orders.

The decision price is a thin proxy. The fund decides on IEX last trades ([fund/broker.py](../fund/broker.py)). IEX handles a small share of volume, half-penny prices in the records point to midpoint prints, and the time of the trade behind each price is not logged, so staleness cannot be measured; the textbook decision price is the NBBO midpoint at decision time (blotter, above). The live record does avoid the most common backtesting error, which is computing a signal from a bar's close and filling at that same close ([BotSpot](https://botspot.trade/guides/look-ahead-bias-and-data-leakage); [TradeLab](https://trytradelab.com/blog/look-ahead-bias-backtesting)). The nightly rebuild applies the logged close−3-minute decisions as taken instead of recomputing them on the close ([PROTOCOL_fund.md](research/PROTOCOL_fund.md)), which is exactly the timestamp discipline TradeLab recommends.

Paired comparisons need matched information. Hoffstein's framework treats otherwise identical books that differ only in schedule as sub-indexes whose dispersion, "often exceeding 100 basis points annualized" in factor indices, is luck rather than skill ([Hoffstein, Faber & Braun](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=3673910)). A fair comparison must therefore differ only in the variable under test, and the Protocol 7B shadow control breaks that rule. For positions it shares with the live book it reuses the live close−20-minute news check, but its own positions are checked at the close in the nightly job that starts at 01:10 UTC ([fund/lifecycle.py, `control_news`](../fund/lifecycle.py); [fund/local/runner.py](../fund/local/runner.py)). Those checks see twenty more minutes of news after a five-hour vendor catch-up; in checks run that late, about a quarter of same-day articles fall in the final two hours before the cutoff, against 1.7% in live checks. The control also checks stops and targets on official closes rather than close−3-minute prices. As the two books diverge under the 7B rules, a growing share of the control's positions get the richer information set, tilting the comparison toward the control by an unknown amount. Any replay of the news checks has the same problem and must filter on the time an article was fetched, not the time it was published.

Timing is not what limits how closely the account tracks the model; share rounding is. Whole-share rounding on a $98k account leaves the account a median **82 bp of NAV** away from the model weights each day. The SPY hedge alone contributes a median 28 bp, because one share is about 0.8% of NAV and a 0.36-share hedge rounds to zero, while timing contributes under 0.1 bp per name. After the close, reconciliation measured a median gross mismatch of 108 bp, including an unexplained 280 bp on 29 October, from positions read at 01:10 UTC, possibly at after-hours marks (`fund-data: lifecycle/live/*.json`, `reconciled` field). Which close the fund scores against is a last measurement question. Practitioners disagree on how often vendor "closes" differ from the primary auction print: Ernie Chan found an 8 bp gap on Apple, and a reader found none across 50 names ([Chan](http://epchan.blogspot.com/2015/04/beware-of-low-frequency-data.html)). Alpaca staff say daily bars come from consolidated trades and point to the auctions endpoint for the official print ([Alpaca forum](https://forum.alpaca.markets/t/open-close-daily-bar-prices-vs-open-close-auction-prices-on-primary-exchange/14227)). The fund's two vendors agree on SPY's close on all ten days it traded.

## Most fixes are measurement; the valuable ones need a new protocol

The repository's rule is mechanical. An operational change must answer "could this change a decision?" with a no and carry a CHANGELOG line; anything else is a new protocol with a restarted clock ([CLAUDE.md](../CLAUDE.md); [fund/CHANGELOG.md](../fund/CHANGELOG.md)). Three precedents calibrate it. The 3 October switch from market-on-close to pre-close market orders was recorded as operational, "no decision input or score changes" ([PROTOCOL_fund.md](research/PROTOCOL_fund.md)), so execution routes have been treated as operational. Protocol 7B changed live decisions and restarted the clock on 28 October. And 7B's first amendment changed decisions but kept the clock, because it was written before any affected position reached the review it changed ([fund/CHANGELOG.md](../fund/CHANGELOG.md)).

## Conclusion

The engineering effort has gone into the last three minutes, where the measured error is about 1% of a position's volatility unit and the measured cost is indistinguishable from zero. The timing that matters sits hours and days upstream, in the vendor's two-to-three-hour lag and the weekly cohort's day-long wait, and in a scorecard that cannot see either one. The cheapest high-value step is therefore not an execution change at all but a log of what the news feed delivers and when, because the largest departure from best practice is currently invisible in the fund's own records.

Turnover reframes the cost question. At about 70 times NAV a year, a single basis point per side is worth about 0.7% a year, which makes both the 5 bp cost assumption (a roughly 3% a year handicap on the scored record) and the closing-auction route (worth up to about 0.7% a year) decisions to take deliberately at go-live, even though every per-trade number looks tiny. The literature's verdict on large-cap news, that it is priced fast and leaves little drift, cuts both ways. It explains why the fund's delays have not visibly hurt it, and it implies that any edge the book has must come from slower, harder reading of news that survives a day's delay, which is exactly what the pre-registered shadow books are positioned to test by April 2027.
